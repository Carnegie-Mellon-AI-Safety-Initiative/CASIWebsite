About the Event

The Carnegie AI Safety Initiative (CASI) at Carnegie Mellon University is hosting the inaugural Pittsburgh AI Policy Hackathon, a hybrid policy writing competition open to undergraduate and graduate students across the greater Pittsburgh area. [cite: 18, 19] Teams of 1–2 will choose from one of three brackets on pressing AI policy challenges, write a policy brief, and top teams will be invited to present in-person to a panel of judges on CMU’s campus. Target Audience: Law, Business, Public Policy, Criminal Justice [cite: 37]

Central Question

How should the federal government assign legal responsibility for AI-enabled consumer fraud across a multi-party deployment chain, and what enforcement mechanism can operate at the speed and scale of autonomous agents rather than human investigators? [cite: 38]

Background

AI-powered voice cloning is being used to run grandparent scams and impersonate bank representatives at a scale no human operation could match. [cite: 39] Current enforcement runs into a structural wall: when an autonomous agent commits fraud, liability is diffused across the developer, the business that deployed it, and the operator. [cite: 40]

Narrowing Questions

  • In the Pittsburgh grandmother scenario, who is the liable party, and what does she actually recover? [cite: 41]
  • Should liability be strict, negligence-based, or tied to specific knowledge? [cite: 41]
  • Which existing statute (FTC Act, TCPA, Wire Fraud) provides the most viable enforcement hook? [cite: 42]
  • How do you distinguish a legitimate small business from a bad actor? [cite: 42]
  • What mechanism closes the gap between human enforcement speed and machine fraud speed? [cite: 43]
  • How do you design victim redress that is accessible to elderly and low-income users? [cite: 43]

Target Audience: CS, Privacy Engineering, IP Law, HCI, Information Science [cite: 46]

Central Question

How should the United States design a prospective licensing framework that compensates creators for future use of their work in AI training, without creating transaction costs that slow development or exclude independent creators? [cite: 47]

Background

Courts are split on whether AI training is fair use. [cite: 48] While institutional rights holders like the AP are reaching private deals, independent creators have no equivalent pathway. [cite: 49] This bracket asks for a framework to govern AI training going forward. [cite: 50]

Narrowing Questions

  • Should the regime be opt-in or opt-out? [cite: 51, 52]
  • What does meaningful disclosure look like at the scale of billions of tokens? [cite: 52]
  • How do you set a statutory royalty rate when the market is still forming? [cite: 52, 53]
  • What is the maximum friction a framework can impose while remaining accessible to solo artists? [cite: 53]
  • How do you handle outputs that mimic a specific artist's style without verbatim reproduction? [cite: 53, 54]

Target Audience: Law, Political Science, Public Policy, Criminal Justice, HCI [cite: 56, 57]

Central Question

How should the federal government limit the use of AI-powered surveillance tools by law enforcement while establishing an oversight mechanism that prevents mission creep and preserves civil liberties? [cite: 57, 58]

Background

U.S. Customs and Border Protection scans travelers without individualized suspicion, despite documented error rates for darker-skinned women. [cite: 58, 59] AI surveillance is qualitatively different from prior generations; it requires a new approach to the Fourth Amendment. [cite: 60]

Narrowing Questions

  • Does the border search exception extend to AI-powered mass biometric surveillance? [cite: 61]
  • Define a specific, bounded category for national security exceptions. [cite: 61]
  • Does technical inaccuracy alone create an equal protection problem? [cite: 62]
  • How do you prevent surveillance tools built for counterterrorism from migrating to target political organizers? [cite: 62]
  • Should federal frameworks preempt or set a floor for municipal bans? [cite: 63]
