Brackets & Prompts

Teams choose one of the three brackets below. Click each bracket to see the full prompt and resources. Your bracket choice is made at registration and is final.

Central Question

How should the federal government assign legal responsibility for AI-enabled consumer fraud across a multi-party deployment chain, and what enforcement mechanism can operate at the speed and scale of autonomous agents rather than human investigators?

Background and Context

Consider a scenario drawn from documented fraud patterns. An elderly woman receives a call from someone who sounds exactly like her grandson. He is stranded and needs $40,000 wired immediately. The voice is a clone, generated by an AI model, deployed by a fraud ring using a commercial agentic AI platform, configured by an operator who has since disappeared. She transfers the money. Law enforcement identifies the agentic system and traces the deployment chain, but no single actor clearly violated an existing criminal statute. The model developer did not know about the fraud. The platform marketed its tools for legitimate sales use. The operator is unreachable. Nobody is prosecuted. The woman has no civil recourse.

AI-powered voice cloning is being used to run grandparent scams, impersonate bank representatives, and conduct fraud at a scale no human operation could match. A single agentic call system can place thousands of simultaneous calls, adapting scripts in real time, at a cost approaching zero per call. The FTC has made clear that using AI tools to deceive or defraud people is illegal under existing law. The agentic AI deployment ecosystem involves at least four distinct commercial actors: developers, platform companies, businesses configuring agents, and consumers. No existing federal statute cleanly assigns liability across this chain.

Your Task

Your team is jointly advising the FTC Bureau of Consumer Protection and the Senate Commerce Committee's consumer protection staff. Develop a federal policy proposal that establishes a liability allocation framework, modifies existing statutes (FTC Act, wire fraud, or TCPA), proposes disclosure and audit requirements, or creates a victim redress mechanism.

Narrowing Questions

  • In the Pittsburgh grandmother scenario, who do you name as the liable party, under what legal theory, and what does she actually recover?
  • Should liability be strict, negligence-based, or tied to specific knowledge of misuse?
  • Which existing statute provides the most viable enforcement hook?
  • How do you design victim redress that is practically accessible to elderly and low-income users?

Relevant Regulations and Resources

  • FTC Act Section 5; Wire Fraud Statute; TCPA (1991); Section 230
  • FTC v. Air AI (2024–2025); FTC Voice Cloning Challenge (2023)
  • Brookings: "Governing AI Agents"; Stanford HAI: "Generative AI and Consumer Fraud"

Central Question

Now that training AI models on existing creative work is an established practice, legally contested but commercially entrenched, how should the United States design a prospective licensing framework that compensates creators for future use of their work, without creating transaction costs that slow AI development or systematically exclude independent creators?

Background and Context

This prompt is not about retroactive liability but what framework should govern AI training going forward. Modern foundation models are trained on datasets containing hundreds of billions of tokens from millions of creators without their knowledge or consent. The copyright question is whether this constitutes fair use under 17 U.S.C. Section 107. The U.S. Copyright Office's May 2025 report concluded that some training uses fall outside fair use and explicitly deferred to Congress for a legislative solution. The private licensing market forming in the absence of legislation establishes compensation rates for institutional rights holders but creates a two-tier system where independent creators have no equivalent pathway.

Your Task

Your team is advising the Senate Judiciary Subcommittee on Intellectual Property. Develop a federal policy proposal that designs a prospective licensing framework, proposes a technical standard for training data provenance and disclosure, creates a mechanism specifically designed to reach independent creators, or proposes a compulsory licensing regime analogous to Section 115.

Narrowing Questions

  • Should a prospective licensing regime be opt-in or opt-out?
  • What does meaningful disclosure look like at this scale, and what is technically feasible?
  • How do you set a statutory royalty rate for AI training use when the market is still forming?
  • What is the maximum friction your framework can impose while remaining genuinely accessible to solo artists?

Relevant Regulations and Resources

  • Copyright Act Section 107 (Fair Use); Copyright Act Section 115 (Compulsory Licensing)
  • U.S. Copyright Office Report on AI and Copyright (May 2025); Bartz v. Anthropic (2025)
  • C2PA / Content Credentials Standard; Mechanical Licensing Collective (MLC)

Central Question

How should the federal government limit the use of AI-powered surveillance tools by law enforcement and intelligence agencies, defining with operational specificity which uses are permissible and which are prohibited, and establishing an institutional mechanism that enforces that boundary?

Background and Context

U.S. Customs and Border Protection currently deploys facial recognition technology at airports and border crossings without individualized suspicion. Technical limitations are central: independent audits have documented facial recognition error rates for darker-skinned women up to 35 percentage points higher than for lighter-skinned men. History shows patterns of surveillance mission creep, from COINTELPRO targeting civil rights leaders to modern tools targeting political organizers far from any port of entry. AI surveillance allows for biometric processing of every person in an airport without any human involvement until a flag is generated.

Your Task

Your team is advising the Senate Judiciary Committee's Subcommittee on Privacy, Technology, and the Law. Develop a federal policy proposal that defines permissible AI surveillance uses, establishes a structural oversight mechanism, proposes technical standards for accuracy and demographic parity, or creates a community accountability mechanism.

Narrowing Questions

  • Does the border search exception extend to AI-powered mass biometric surveillance?
  • Define the national security exception your framework permits as a specific, bounded category.
  • Does technical inaccuracy alone create a constitutional equal protection problem?
  • How does your framework prevent surveillance tools built for counterterrorism from migrating to target political organizers?

Relevant Regulations and Resources

  • Fourth Amendment; FISA and Section 702; Executive Order 12333
  • EU AI Act Article 5 (2024): prohibition on real-time biometric surveillance
  • Church Committee Final Report (1975–76); MIT Media Lab: "Gender Shades" (2018)

Submission Guidelines

Your submission is a policy brief responding to one of the three prompts. You will choose one track when you register, and this choice is final.

What to Submit

A policy brief of under 1,500 words including:

  • Executive summary
  • A clear statement of the problem
  • Your proposed policy solution
  • Implementation considerations
  • Anticipated objections or tradeoffs
Formatting
  • PDF format; 1-inch margins
  • 12pt Times New Roman; 1.15 line spacing
  • Under 1,500 words (references not counted)
  • Include your name(s) and bracket number in the header
  • Do not include names in the document itself — submissions are reviewed anonymously.
Citations & AI Tool Use

Please cite your sources in any standard format (APA, Chicago, etc.). References do not count toward the word limit. You may use AI tools for research and editing assistance, but the ideas and arguments must be your own. Include a brief disclosure at the end of your brief noting any AI tools used.

How to Submit

Submit your policy brief as a PDF through the submission form here. Only one person per team needs to submit. Deadline: Wednesday, April 15 at 11:59 PM ET. Only the most recent submission will be reviewed.

Judging Rubric

  • Problem analysis: Do you demonstrate a clear understanding of the issue?
  • Policy solution: Is your proposal specific, well-designed, and actionable?
  • Feasibility: Is it politically, legally, and practically viable?
  • Anticipation of objections: Do you address counterarguments and tradeoffs?
  • Clarity and persuasiveness: Is the brief well-written and well-organized?
