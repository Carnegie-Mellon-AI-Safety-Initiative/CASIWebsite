Target Audience: Law, Business, Public Policy, Criminal Justice

Central Question

How should the federal government assign legal responsibility for AI-enabled consumer fraud across a multi-party deployment chain, and what enforcement mechanism can operate at the speed and scale of autonomous agents rather than human investigators?

Background and Context

AI-powered voice cloning is being used to run grandparent scams, impersonate bank representatives, and conduct fraud at a scale no human operation could match. A single agentic call system can place thousands of simultaneous calls, adapting scripts in real time, at a cost approaching zero per call.

The FTC has made clear that using AI tools to deceive or defraud people is illegal under existing law. However, when an autonomous agent commits fraud, liability is diffused across the developer who built the model, the business that deployed it, and the operator who configured it. No existing federal statute cleanly assigns liability across this chain.

Narrowing Questions

  • In the Pittsburgh grandmother scenario, who do you name as the liable party, under what legal theory, and what does she actually recover?
  • Should liability be strict, negligence-based, or tied to specific knowledge of misuse?
  • Which existing statute provides the most viable enforcement hook?
  • How does your framework distinguish a small business using legitimate agentic AI from a bad actor?
  • What mechanism closes the gap between human enforcement speed and machine fraud speed?
  • How do you design victim redress that is practically accessible to elderly and low-income users?

Target Audience: CS, Privacy Engineering, IP Law, HCI, IS

Central Question

How should the United States design a prospective licensing framework that compensates creators for future use of their work in AI training, without creating transaction costs that slow development or exclude independent creators?

Background and Context

Courts are currently split on whether AI training constitutes fair use. While institutional rights holders are beginning to secure private licensing deals, independent creators are being left behind. The U.S. Copyright Office has deferred to Congress for a legislative solution to resolve the legal and policy conflict over training data.

Narrowing Questions

  • Should a prospective licensing regime be opt-in or opt-out? What are the equity implications for independent creators?
  • What does meaningful disclosure look like at the scale of hundreds of billions of tokens?
  • How do you set a statutory royalty rate for AI training when the market is still forming?
  • What is the maximum friction your framework can impose while remaining genuinely accessible to solo artists?
  • How does your framework handle AI systems that generate outputs "in the style of" an artist without verbatim reproduction?

Target Audience: Law, Political Science, Public Policy, Criminal Justice, HCI

Central Question

How should the federal government limit the use of AI-powered surveillance tools by law enforcement while establishing an institutional mechanism that prevents mission creep and preserves civil liberties?

Background and Context

CBP currently deploys facial recognition at borders without individualized suspicion, despite documented error rates for darker-skinned individuals. AI surveillance is qualitatively different; it allows for continuous, automated monitoring that traditional constitutional frameworks were not designed to handle.

Narrowing Questions

  • Does the border search exception extend to AI-powered mass biometric surveillance?
  • Define a specific, bounded category for national security exceptions. How do you prevent it from expanding?
  • Does technical inaccuracy alone create a constitutional equal protection problem?
  • How do you prevent counterterrorism tools from being used to target political organizers?
  • Should federal frameworks preempt or set a floor for municipal bans in cities like San Francisco?

Submission Guidelines

Formatting

  • PDF format, 12pt Times New Roman, 1.15 line spacing.
  • Under 1,500 words (references not counted).
  • Anonymized: Do not include names inside the document.
