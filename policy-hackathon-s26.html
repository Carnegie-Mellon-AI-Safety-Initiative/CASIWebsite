Central Question

How should the federal government assign legal responsibility for AI-enabled consumer fraud across a multi-party deployment chain, and what enforcement mechanism can operate at the speed and scale of autonomous agents rather than human investigators?

Background and Context

Consider a scenario drawn from documented fraud patterns. An elderly woman receives a call from someone who sounds exactly like her grandson. He is stranded and needs $40,000 wired immediately. The voice is a clone, generated by an AI model, deployed by a fraud ring using a commercial agentic AI platform, configured by an operator who has since disappeared. She transfers the money. Law enforcement identifies the agentic system and traces the deployment chain, but no single actor clearly violated an existing criminal statute. The model developer did not know about the fraud. The platform marketed its tools for legitimate sales use. The operator is unreachable. Nobody is prosecuted. The woman has no civil recourse.

This is not a hypothetical edge case. AI-powered voice cloning is being used to run grandparent scams, impersonate bank representatives, and conduct fraud at a scale no human operation could match. A single agentic call system can place thousands of simultaneous calls, adapting scripts in real time, at a cost approaching zero per call. The FTC has made clear that using AI tools to deceive or defraud people is illegal under existing law. Its enforcement action against Air AI, which marketed agentic tools capable of autonomously conducting sales calls without disclosing the AI nature of interactions, may be the first consumer protection case to allege deception specifically in the marketing of agentic AI capabilities. But enforcement keeps running into the same structural wall.

The agentic AI deployment ecosystem involves at least four distinct commercial actors. AI model and voice cloning developers build and license the underlying systems. Platform companies package these capabilities into deployable agentic products. Businesses and operators configure agent behavior, including scripts, targets, and escalation logic, and deploy them in consumer-facing contexts. Consumers and victims often have no way to verify whether they are interacting with a human or an autonomous system. No existing federal statute cleanly assigns liability across this chain. The FTC Act Section 5 prohibits unfair or deceptive acts in commerce but assumes identifiable responsible parties. Wire fraud statutes require proof of intent, which is difficult to establish when the proximate actor is an autonomous system. Section 230 may shield platform intermediaries from liability for third-party agent outputs. The result is a system where each actor has plausible grounds to disclaim responsibility and victims have no clear recourse.

The Telephone Consumer Protection Act of 1991 offers a relevant precedent. When robocall technology enabled fraud at a scale human enforcement could not match, Congress responded with structural restrictions on the technology itself, rather than trying to prosecute every bad actor.

Your Task

Your team is jointly advising the FTC Bureau of Consumer Protection and the Senate Commerce Committee's consumer protection staff. Develop a federal policy proposal that establishes a liability allocation framework, modifies existing statutes (FTC Act, wire fraud, or TCPA), proposes disclosure/audit requirements, or creates a victim redress mechanism.

Narrowing Questions

  • In the scenario described, who do you name as the liable party and under what theory?
  • Should liability be strict, negligence-based, or tied to specific knowledge?
  • Existing enforcement tools operate at human speed; AI fraud operates at machine speed. What mechanism closes this gap?
  • How do you design victim redress that is practically accessible to elderly and low-income users?

Relevant Regulations and Resources

  • FTC Act Section 5; Wire Fraud Statute; TCPA (1991); Section 230
  • FTC v. Air AI (2024–2025); FTC Voice Cloning Challenge (2023)
  • Brookings: "Governing AI Agents"; Stanford HAI: "Generative AI and Consumer Fraud"

Central Question

Now that training AI models on existing creative work is an established practice, legally contested but commercially entrenched, how should the United States design a prospective licensing framework that compensates creators for future use of their work, without creating transaction costs that slow AI development or systematically exclude independent creators?

Background and Context

This prompt asks a different and more urgent question: what framework should govern AI training going forward? Modern foundation models are trained on datasets containing hundreds of billions of tokens from millions of creators. The copyright question is whether this constitutes fair use under 17 U.S.C. Section 107. The U.S. Copyright Office's May 2025 report concluded that some training uses fall outside fair use and deferred to Congress for a legislative solution.

Congress has a relevant precedent in the Copyright Act of 1909 which established a compulsory licensing regime for mechanical reproduction. Whether a similar structure is appropriate for AI training data is a central question. The private licensing market forming in the absence of legislation establishes compensation rates for institutional rights holders but creates a two-tier system where independent creators have no equivalent pathway.

Your Task

Your team is advising the Senate Judiciary Subcommittee on Intellectual Property. Develop a federal policy proposal that designs a prospective licensing framework specifying the default rule, proposes a technical standard for provenance, or creates a mechanism specifically designed to reach independent creators.

Relevant Regulations and Resources

  • Copyright Act Section 107 and Section 115; Music Modernization Act (2018)
  • U.S. Copyright Office Report on AI and Copyright (May 2025)
  • C2PA / Content Credentials Standard; Mechanical Licensing Collective (MLC)

Central Question

How should the federal government limit the use of AI-powered surveillance tools by law enforcement and intelligence agencies, defining with operational specificity which uses are permissible and which are prohibited, and establishing an institutional mechanism that enforces that boundary?

Background and Context

U.S. Customs and Border Protection scans travelers without individualized suspicion. Technical limitations are central: independent audits have documented facial recognition error rates for darker-skinned women up to 35 percentage points higher than for lighter-skinned men. History shows patterns of surveillance mission creep, from COINTELPRO targeting civil rights leaders to modern tools targeting political organizers far from any port of entry. AI surveillance is qualitatively different, allowing for biometric processing of every person in an airport without any human involvement until a flag is generated.

Your Task

Your team is advising the Senate Judiciary Committee's Subcommittee on Privacy, Technology, and the Law. Develop a federal policy proposal that defines permissible AI surveillance uses, establishes a structural oversight mechanism, proposes technical standards for accuracy, or creates a community accountability mechanism.

Submission Guidelines

What to Submit

A policy brief of under 1,500 words including: Executive summary, clear statement of problem, proposed policy solution, implementation considerations, and anticipated tradeoffs.

Formatting
  • PDF format; 1-inch margins
  • 12pt Times New Roman; 1.15 line spacing
  • Under 1,500 words (references not counted)
  • Include your name(s) and bracket number in the header ONLY
Citations & AI Tool Use

Cite sources in any standard format. You may use AI tools for assistance, but the ideas and arguments must be your own; include a brief disclosure noting any AI tools used.

How to Submit

Submit via the submission form here. Deadline: Wednesday, April 15 at 11:59 PM ET. Only the most recent submission will be reviewed.

Judging Rubric

  • Problem analysis: Clear understanding of the issue?
  • Policy solution: Specific and actionable?
  • Feasibility: Legally and practically viable?
  • Anticipation of objections: Addressed counterarguments?
  • Clarity: Well-written and organized?
