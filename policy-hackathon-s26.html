Central Question

How should the federal government assign legal responsibility for AI-enabled consumer fraud across a multi-party deployment chain, and what enforcement mechanism can operate at the speed and scale of autonomous agents rather than human investigators?

Background and Context

Consider a scenario drawn from documented fraud patterns. An elderly woman receives a call from someone who sounds exactly like her grandson. He is stranded and needs $40,000 wired immediately. The voice is a clone, generated by an AI model, deployed by a fraud ring using a commercial agentic AI platform, configured by an operator who has since disappeared. She transfers the money. Law enforcement identifies the agentic system and traces the deployment chain, but no single actor clearly violated an existing criminal statute. The model developer did not know about the fraud. The platform marketed its tools for legitimate sales use. The operator is unreachable. Nobody is prosecuted. The woman has no civil recourse.

This is not a hypothetical edge case. AI-powered voice cloning is being used to run grandparent scams, impersonate bank representatives, and conduct fraud at a scale no human operation could match. A single agentic call system can place thousands of simultaneous calls, adapting scripts in real time, at a cost approaching zero per call.

The FTC has made clear that using AI tools to deceive or defraud people is illegal under existing law. Its enforcement action against Air AI, which marketed agentic tools capable of autonomously conducting sales calls without disclosing the AI nature of interactions, may be the first consumer protection case to allege deception specifically in the marketing of agentic AI capabilities. But enforcement keeps running into the same structural wall.

The agentic AI deployment ecosystem involves at least four distinct commercial actors. AI model and voice cloning developers build and license the underlying systems. Platform companies package these capabilities into deployable agentic products. Businesses and operators configure agent behavior, including scripts, targets, and escalation logic, and deploy them in consumer-facing contexts. Consumers and victims often have no way to verify whether they are interacting with a human or an autonomous system.

No existing federal statute cleanly assigns liability across this chain. The FTC Act Section 5 prohibits unfair or deceptive acts in commerce but assumes identifiable responsible parties. Wire fraud statutes require proof of intent, which is difficult to establish when the proximate actor is an autonomous system. Section 230 may shield platform intermediaries from liability for third-party agent outputs. The result is a system where each actor has plausible grounds to disclaim responsibility and victims have no clear recourse.

The liability gap does not only harm victims. It also creates compliance uncertainty for businesses deploying agentic AI in legitimate customer service, financial services, and healthcare applications. Without a clear federal standard, businesses face unpredictable exposure and no guidance on what responsible deployment looks like.

The Telephone Consumer Protection Act of 1991 offers a relevant precedent. When robocall technology enabled fraud at a scale human enforcement could not match, Congress responded with structural restrictions on the technology itself, including auto-dialer prohibitions and carrier-level obligations, rather than trying to prosecute every bad actor. The AI fraud moment is structurally similar.

Your Task

Your team is jointly advising the FTC Bureau of Consumer Protection and the Senate Commerce Committee's consumer protection staff. Develop a federal policy proposal that does one or more of the following:

  • Establishes a liability allocation framework that assigns responsibility across the agentic AI deployment chain under a coherent legal theory
  • Modifies existing statutes, including the FTC Act, wire fraud, or TCPA, to address agentic AI fraud without requiring entirely new legislation
  • Proposes disclosure and audit requirements for businesses deploying agentic AI in consumer contexts, including what meaningful disclosure looks like at deployment scale
  • Creates a victim redress mechanism that gives consumers practical recourse when agentic AI fraud causes financial harm

Narrowing Questions

  • In the Pittsburgh grandmother scenario described above, who do you name as the liable party, under what legal theory, and what does she actually recover? Walk through the chain and explain where liability attaches and where it does not.
  • Should liability be strict (no intent required), negligence-based (what a reasonable deployer should have known), or tied to specific knowledge of misuse? What are the deterrence and innovation tradeoffs of each standard?
  • Your framework must be implementable under existing congressional authority or identify a specific statutory hook. Assume Congress will not pass entirely new AI-specific legislation in the current session. Which existing statute provides the most viable enforcement hook?
  • A small business using an off-the-shelf agentic AI platform for customer service faces the same liability ambiguity as a bad actor using the same platform for fraud. How does your framework distinguish between them, and what compliance pathway does the legitimate small business have?
  • Existing enforcement tools operate at human speed. AI-enabled fraud operates at machine speed, with thousands of calls possible before any enforcement action. What mechanism closes this gap?
  • How do you design victim redress that is practically accessible to the populations most harmed, including elderly consumers and low-income users who are the least likely to navigate civil litigation?

Relevant Regulations and Resources

  • FTC Act Section 5: unfair or deceptive acts or practices in commerce
  • Wire Fraud Statute (18 U.S.C. Section 1343): federal criminal fraud and intent requirements
  • Telephone Consumer Protection Act (1991): robocall structural restrictions and the closest legislative precedent
  • Section 230, Communications Decency Act: platform liability shield and applicability to agentic AI outputs
  • CFPB Circular on AI in Financial Products (2024)
  • FTC v. Air AI (2024–2025): first consumer protection action alleging deceptive marketing of agentic AI capabilities
  • FTC Voice Cloning Challenge (2023): formal acknowledgment of enforcement gap for synthetic voice fraud
  • FTC Impersonation Rule (2024): extended impersonation protections to AI-generated voice and likeness
  • Brookings: "Governing AI Agents" (2024): liability frameworks for multi-principal agentic systems
  • Stanford HAI: "Generative AI and Consumer Fraud" (2024): empirical landscape of AI-enabled consumer harm

Central Question

Now that training AI models on existing creative work is an established practice, legally contested but commercially entrenched, how should the United States design a prospective licensing framework that compensates creators for future use of their work, without creating transaction costs that slow AI development or systematically exclude independent creators in favor of institutional rights holders who have the leverage to negotiate private deals?

Background and Context

This prompt is not about the legal battles over past training. Those questions are working through courts in cases including Bartz v. Anthropic, Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence, and Getty Images v. Stability AI. A legislative framework designed primarily to resolve retroactive liability would be hostage to litigation outcomes that may take years to settle. This prompt asks a different and more urgent question: what framework should govern AI training going forward?

Modern foundation models are trained on datasets assembled from web crawls, licensed corpora, and third-party data sources containing hundreds of billions of tokens. At this scale, training datasets routinely include the work of millions of individual creators without their knowledge or consent. The specific creators whose work was included, at what weight, and with what influence on model outputs are questions that current technical architectures often cannot answer precisely. Any licensing framework must engage with what is technically feasible at this scale.

The copyright question at the center of this prompt is whether AI training on existing works constitutes fair use under 17 U.S.C. Section 107. Courts have reached inconsistent conclusions. Bartz v. Anthropic found training on copyrighted books to be transformatively fair use. Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence found the opposite for legal research content, emphasizing that AI outputs directly substituted for the original product's market. The U.S. Copyright Office's May 2025 report concluded that some training uses fall outside fair use and explicitly deferred to Congress for a legislative solution.

Congress has a relevant precedent worth examining closely. When player pianos threatened sheet music composers in the early twentieth century, Congress did not require one-to-one consent for every mechanical reproduction. Instead, the Copyright Act of 1909 established a compulsory licensing regime: anyone could record a cover of a previously released song without the original artist's permission, provided they paid a statutory royalty. Section 115 of the current Copyright Act preserves this mechanism. Whether a similar structure is appropriate for AI training data, and how to design it so that independent creators without institutional representation can actually participate, is a central question your brief should address.

The private licensing market forming in the absence of legislation illustrates the stakes. Universal Music Group, Sony Music, and Warner Music have each reached licensing agreements with AI music platforms. The Associated Press and several major newspaper groups have licensed their archives to OpenAI. These deals establish compensation rates for institutional rights holders but create a two-tier system where independent creators have no equivalent pathway. If Congress does not act before this market hardens, a future legislative framework will face incumbents who have locked in favorable terms and have every incentive to resist a more inclusive system. The window to design an inclusive licensing ecosystem is closing.

Your Task

Your team is advising the Senate Judiciary Subcommittee on Intellectual Property, which is preparing draft legislation for the 2026 session and has set aside the retroactive liability question. Develop a federal policy proposal that does one or more of the following:

  • Designs a prospective licensing framework specifying the default rule (opt-in vs. opt-out), the rate-setting mechanism, and the administrative body responsible for royalty distribution
  • Proposes a technical standard for training data provenance and disclosure that is feasible at scale and meaningful for creators trying to determine whether their work was used
  • Creates a mechanism specifically designed to reach independent creators, not just institutional rights holders, including how they register, how they receive payment, and what the barrier to participation looks like
  • Proposes a compulsory licensing regime analogous to Section 115, specifying what it permits, what it prohibits, and how it handles creators who affirmatively opt out

Teams with a technical background are encouraged to engage with the system design question directly: what infrastructure, including provenance databases, opt-out registries, and royalty distribution APIs, would your framework require? Is it buildable with current technology, and what are the failure modes?

Teams with an HCI or information science background are encouraged to engage with the creator experience question: what does your framework look like from the perspective of an independent visual artist in Pittsburgh who wants to know whether her work was used to train an image generation model, and who wants to be compensated if it was?

Narrowing Questions

  • Should a prospective licensing regime be opt-in (creators must register to receive compensation) or opt-out (creators are automatically included unless they withdraw)? What are the equity implications of each default, specifically for independent creators without legal or administrative resources?
  • Modern training datasets contain hundreds of billions of tokens from millions of creators. What does meaningful disclosure look like at this scale, and what is technically feasible? Is there a difference between disclosing dataset composition at the category level and at the individual creator level?
  • How do you set a statutory royalty rate for AI training use when the market for that use is still forming? Section 115 rates are set by the Copyright Royalty Board through a formal proceeding. What equivalent mechanism applies here, and who has standing to participate?
  • Your framework must reach independent creators without institutional representation. An independent musician has no label to negotiate on her behalf, no legal department, and no database of her works in a standardized format. What is the maximum friction your framework can impose while remaining genuinely accessible?
  • How does your framework handle AI systems that generate outputs in the style of a specific artist without reproducing their work verbatim? Is that a training data problem, an output problem, or neither?

Relevant Regulations and Resources

  • Copyright Act Section 107 (Fair Use): four-factor test and the central legal battleground for training data
  • Copyright Act Section 115 (Compulsory Licensing): mechanical licensing for musical compositions and the primary legislative analogue
  • Digital Millennium Copyright Act (DMCA): opt-out and safe harbor provisions and limitations for AI-scale ingestion
  • Music Modernization Act (2018): most recent update to Section 115; created the Mechanical Licensing Collective as a model for collective administration
  • U.S. Copyright Office Report on AI and Copyright (May 2025): Congress explicitly tasked with prospective framework
  • Bartz v. Anthropic (N.D. Cal. 2025): fair use found for book training
  • Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence (D. Del. 2025): fair use rejected for legal content
  • Authors Guild v. OpenAI (ongoing): class action on behalf of individual authors
  • C2PA / Content Credentials Standard: technical standard for content provenance and disclosure feasibility
  • Mechanical Licensing Collective (MLC): existing collective administration model under Section 115
  • Copyright Alliance: "AI Training and Creator Compensation" (2024): creator-side framework
  • Electronic Frontier Foundation: "Fair Use and AI Training" (2024): developer-side framework
  • EU Text and Data Mining Exception (DSM Directive Article 4): comparative opt-out framework

Central Question

How should the federal government limit the use of AI-powered surveillance tools by law enforcement and intelligence agencies, defining with operational specificity which uses are permissible and which are prohibited, and establishing an institutional mechanism that enforces that boundary and prevents exceptions from expanding over time, while preserving legitimate national security applications?

Background and Context

U.S. Customs and Border Protection currently deploys facial recognition technology at airports, land border crossings, and seaports, scanning travelers including U.S. citizens and cross-referencing them against federal databases without individualized suspicion. CBP has also used AI tools to monitor social media and build behavioral profiles in border enforcement operations. Under the border search exception to the Fourth Amendment, courts have permitted warrantless searches at the border without individualized suspicion. Whether this exception extends to AI-powered mass biometric surveillance remains constitutionally unsettled.

The technical limitations of these systems are central to the policy problem, not peripheral to it. Independent audits have documented facial recognition error rates for darker-skinned women up to 35 percentage points higher than for lighter-skinned men. In 2023, Robert Williams of Detroit became one of at least three documented cases of wrongful arrest based on facial recognition misidentification. A system that misidentifies one demographic at significantly higher rates raises equal protection questions independent of any warrant requirement.

The history of surveillance mission creep is directly relevant. COINTELPRO operated under national security authority from 1956 to 1971 and was used primarily against civil rights leaders and political organizers. The Church Committee reforms of 1975 established the oversight architecture, including FISA and the FISC, that still governs national security surveillance today. Each subsequent modification was presented as narrow and necessary. Each expanded the surveillance architecture. AI surveillance tools initially deployed at the border have since been documented targeting Black Lives Matter protests, Muslim communities, and immigration advocates far from any port of entry.

AI surveillance is qualitatively different from prior generations. A single system can process the biometric data of every person passing through a major airport and flag individuals for review without any human involvement until a flag is generated. Constitutional frameworks designed for surveillance that required human effort at scale do not map cleanly onto systems that face no such constraint.

Your Task

Your team is advising the Senate Judiciary Committee's Subcommittee on Privacy, Technology, and the Law. Develop a federal policy proposal that does one or more of the following:

  • Defines with operational specificity which AI surveillance uses are permissible and which are prohibited, including at the border where constitutional protections are weakest
  • Establishes an oversight mechanism, whether judicial, legislative, or administrative, with a structural design that resists historical patterns of exception creep
  • Proposes technical standards, including accuracy, demographic parity, and audit requirements, that AI surveillance systems must meet before deployment, and specifies what happens when a deployed system falls below those standards
  • Creates a community accountability mechanism that gives affected populations a role in surveillance governance beyond litigation

Narrowing Questions

  • Does the border search exception extend to AI-powered mass biometric surveillance of every person at a port of entry? If courts have not resolved this, what standard should Congress establish and on what constitutional basis?
  • Define the national security exception your framework permits. Not "legitimate national security purposes" but a specific, bounded category of uses. What institutional mechanism prevents that exception from expanding, and what does the historical record suggest about the durability of narrow exceptions?
  • Does technical inaccuracy alone, without discriminatory intent, create a constitutional equal protection problem? What accuracy and demographic parity standards should apply before a system is cleared for government use?
  • AI surveillance tools built for counterterrorism have been documented targeting political organizers and advocacy communities. How does your framework prevent that migration, and who has standing to challenge a violation?
  • Should a federal framework preempt more protective municipal bans in San Francisco, Boston, and Portland, preserve them, or set a floor that localities can exceed? What are the federalism implications?

Relevant Regulations and Resources

  • Fourth Amendment; Almeida-Sanchez v. United States (1973); Carpenter v. United States (2018)
  • Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act (FISA) and Section 702
  • Executive Order 12333
  • Facial Recognition and Biometric Technology Moratorium Act (introduced, not enacted)
  • EU AI Act Article 5 (2024): prohibition on real-time biometric surveillance; comparative model
  • CBP Facial Recognition Privacy Impact Assessments (2020–2024)
  • Church Committee Final Report (1975–76): COINTELPRO findings and mission creep precedent
  • MIT Media Lab: "Gender Shades" (Buolamwini and Gebru, 2018): accuracy disparities by race and gender
  • Georgetown Law: "The Perpetual Line-Up" (2016): law enforcement facial recognition landscape
  • Brennan Center: "AI and Government Surveillance" (2024)
  • ACLU: "Face Recognition Technology and CBP" (2023)

Submission Guidelines

Your submission is a policy brief responding to one of the three prompts. You will choose one track when you register, and this choice is final.

What to Submit

A policy brief of under 1,500 words (excluding references). Your brief should include:

  • An executive summary
  • A clear statement of the problem
  • Your proposed policy solution
  • Implementation considerations (who implements it, how, what it costs, what the timeline looks like)
  • Anticipated objections or tradeoffs, and how you’d address them

Formatting

  • PDF format; 1-inch margins
  • 12pt Times New Roman; 1.15 line spacing
  • Under 1,500 words (references not counted)
  • Include your name(s) and bracket number in the header. Do not include your names in the document itself — submissions are reviewed anonymously.
  • File name: Member1FullName_Member2FullName_Bracket1.pdf
Citations & AI Tool Use

Please cite your sources in any standard format (APA, Chicago, etc.). References do not count toward the word limit. You may use AI tools for research and editing assistance, but the ideas and arguments must be your own. Include a brief disclosure at the end of your brief noting any AI tools used.

How to Submit

Submit your policy brief as a PDF through the submission form here. Only one person per team needs to submit. Deadline: Wednesday, April 15 at 11:59 PM ET. Only the most recent version will be reviewed.

Judging Rubric

  • Problem analysis: Do you demonstrate a clear understanding of the issue?
  • Policy solution: Is your proposal specific, well-designed, and actionable?
  • Feasibility: Is it politically, legally, and practically viable?
  • Anticipation of objections: Do you address counterarguments and tradeoffs?
  • Clarity and persuasiveness: Is the brief well-written and well-organized?
